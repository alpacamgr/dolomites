The Triassic is the heart of the Dolomites' story, and the globe cannot do it justice. What it can show is where the region sat. Continents and the marker come from the PALEOMAP model of Scotese & Wright 2018, the same reconstruction behind the PaleoDEM; the model draws plate boundaries only for the last hundred million years, so none appear here [@scotese-2018]. The current international time scale places the base of the Middle Triassic just after 247 million years ago [@ics-2026].

Adria drifts across the northern tropics, but exactly where is contested. For 240 million years ago the displayed model puts the Dolomites at about seven degrees north [@scotese-2018]. Two other global models, computed for this site from their published rotation files, put the same point between about one degree (Müller et al. 2019) and about twelve (Merdith et al. 2021) [@muller-2019; @merdith-2021]. Paleomagnetic measurements from the Seceda section, in the Dolomites themselves, give sixteen to eighteen [@muttoni-1997]. The models use different reference frames, and paleomagnetic latitudes carry their own uncertainty.

Above the moving marker, a warm shallow arm of Tethys spreads out. On this shelf, carbonate platforms grow into towers. Deep basins settle black hemipelagic muds. Volcanoes cut briefly through. None of it is visible on the globe: a single grid cell of the high-resolution PaleoDEM is about the size of the Sella group. The reefs belong to the next chapter, which switches to the terrain [@bosellini-2003].
