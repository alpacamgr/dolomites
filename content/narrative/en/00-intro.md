You are looking at the Dolomites as they exist today. The relief is measured, not painted: inside Italy it comes from the ten-metre national elevation model TINITALY published by INGV [@tarquini-2023]; where the view drifts across the border the site falls back to the thirty-metre global Copernicus DEM GLO-30 [@copernicus-dem]. The colour on the rocks is not decoration either. It comes from open geological maps: the provincial maps of South Tyrol, released into the public domain by the province of Bolzano [@bolzano-geologia], and of Trentino, published by the province's geological survey [@pat-geologia]; and, for the Belluno side, the much coarser 1:250,000 lithology map of the Veneto region [@veneto-litologia]. Where those have no open data, the site uses swisstopo's GeoCover for Switzerland [@swisstopo-geocover], the older and coarser 1:100,000 geological map of Italy by ISPRA, surveyed between 1880 and 1980, for the remaining Italian areas [@ispra-geologia-100k], and GeoSphere Austria's 1:500,000 map for Austria [@geosphere-ge-500k]. Each rock unit is coloured by its age on the international time scale. Light grey marks rock that is mapped but has no age given, or none reliable enough to colour; hatching marks areas with no open geological map.

From here, if you scroll upward, time runs backward. Three hundred million years of it. The story alternates between two stages. A globe carries the plate-tectonic chapters, because plate motion only makes sense at that scale. This same terrain, coloured for what the rock is, carries the local chapters: the Triassic reefs, the Alpine collision, the ice ages, and the present.

Everything on the screen carries one of four honesty labels. Observed is measured today. Modeled comes from a published numerical model. Interpreted is a researcher's map drawn from field evidence. Illustrative is drawing. If a fact has no source, it does not appear.
