The Dolomites sit inside a young thrust belt without being crushed by it. Three great faults frame them. To the north runs the Periadriatic-Insubric line, the Alpine transpressive backbone. To the west, the Giudicarie fault system offsets that line and reactivates in the late Miocene. To the south, the Valsugana thrust system drives the whole block upward [@bosellini-2003]. The fault traces on the terrain come from INGV's DISS 3.3.1 database [@diss-3-3-1].

The main uplift here is Miocene. Apatite fission-track and (U-Th)/He data narrow the principal Valsugana-phase shortening and cooling in the Dolomites to roughly seventeen to nine million years ago [@zattin-2006].

What kept the Triassic architecture visible is that this deformation stayed thin- to thick-skinned and only lightly metamorphic. Unlike the Central and Western Alps, the Dolomites were tilted and lifted rather than crumpled, and the reef-and-basin geometries survive as scenery [@bosellini-2003]. The push has not stopped: the ALPS2017 GNSS grid records about one to one and a half millimetres a year of vertical rise across the Dolomites sector today [@sanchez-2018].
