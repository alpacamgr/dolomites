The Dolomites sit inside a young thrust belt without being crushed by it. Three great faults frame them. To the north runs the Periadriatic-Insubric line, the Alpine dextral-transpressive backbone. To the west, the Giudicarie fault system offsets that line and reactivates in the late Miocene. To the south, the Valsugana thrust system drives the whole block upward [@bosellini-2003]. Fault traces on the terrain come from the DISS 3.3.1 database, released by INGV [@diss-3-3-1].

The main uplift here is Miocene. Apatite fission-track and (U-Th)/He data narrow the principal Valsugana-phase shortening and cooling in the Dolomites to roughly 17 to 9 million years ago [@zattin-2006].

What kept the Triassic architecture visible is that this deformation was thin- to thick-skinned and only lightly metamorphic. Unlike the Central and Western Alps, the Dolomites were tilted and lifted rather than crumpled, which is why the reef-and-basin geometries survive as scenery [@bosellini-2003]. The push has not stopped: the ALPS2017 GNSS grid records about 1.0 to 1.5 millimetres a year of vertical rise across the Dolomites sector today [@sanchez-2018].
