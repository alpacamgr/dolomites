The Middle Triassic reefs are not a reconstruction on this screen. They are the mountains themselves. In the Anisian and Ladinian, isolated carbonate platforms grew on the shallow Tethyan shelf, separated by deep basins that filled with dark hemipelagic muds and volcaniclastic turbidites [@bosellini-2003]. Their names are still on the peaks: Schlern/Sciliar, Latemar, Sella, Marmolada, Rosengarten/Catinaccio, Civetta and others. Because the Dolomites were only lightly deformed later, the platform-and-basin geometry survives essentially intact. Colouring the modern terrain by rock unit literally draws the reefs on the summits that carry their names.

The Latemar is the classic case, listed by the IUGS as a global geoheritage site [@iugs-latemar]. Reconstructions of the Schlern/Sciliar platform give it about eight hundred metres of preserved thickness, a proxy for the maximum reef-to-basin relief in the western Dolomites [@marangon-2021].

How long the platforms took to build is contested. Cyclostratigraphy of Milankovitch precession bands gives the Latemar at least nine million years; U-Pb ages on the tuffs interbedded with the reef allow only one to two. The Latemar controversy is unresolved [@iugs-latemar].

Around the growing reefs, high-precision zircon U-Pb ages show that the main pulse of Middle Triassic magmatism in the Southern Alps ran for at least five million years: silicic eruptions first, then, in under a million years, voluminous basaltic lava flows, and a final set of silicic tuffs at the end [@storck-2019].
