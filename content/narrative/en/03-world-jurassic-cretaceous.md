Pangea breaks. A narrow ocean opens between Europe and Adria: the Alpine Tethys, with its western arm the Piemont-Liguria basin. The globe shows this age with the PALEOMAP model of Scotese & Wright 2018, which draws plate boundaries only for the last hundred million years, so they appear only in the later part of this chapter [@scotese-2018]. The Alpine detail comes from a separate regional reconstruction. Le Breton et al. 2021, layered onto the Müller et al. 2019 backbone, treat Adria as two plates—an Ivrea piece in the north, an Apulian piece in the south—and reconstruct the basin's opening in four stages between 200 and 83 million years ago [@lebreton-2021; @muller-2019].

The mature Piemont-Liguria ocean is not the sprawling Tethys of school atlases. In the Le Breton model it holds, between Adria and Europe, at most about 250 kilometres of true oceanic crust [@lebreton-2021]. Other reconstructions draw it wider or narrower, and paleomagnetic uncertainty covers several hundred kilometres either way.

Under the water, the Dolomites are the passive margin: they drown. The Bosellini, Gianolla and Stefani synthesis reads the region's Permian-to-Cretaceous succession as a Tethyan passive-margin sequence, with the Middle Jurassic marking the oceanic break-up [@bosellini-2003]. White Biancone chalks and red Ammonitico limestones settle in the deep water; there are no more reefs.
