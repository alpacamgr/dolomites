Three hundred million years ago the Earth's land had gathered into a single supercontinent, Pangea. The globe below replays the PALEOMAP plate model of Scotese & Wright 2018, one reconstruction for the whole span from 300 million years ago to today. The coarse land-and-sea texture behind the plates, the PaleoDEM at its native five-million-year steps, comes from the same reconstruction. The model draws plate boundaries only for the last 100 million years, so none appear in this chapter [@scotese-2018].

The marker for the future Dolomites sits well inside Pangea, close to the equator, on the piece of shelf we now call Adria. Paleomagnetic data show that this margin, including the Dolomites, has moved in close conjunction with Africa since at least Permian times [@muttoni-2001]. The rocks under the marker are the eroded roots of the older Variscan mountains, and, resting on them, the first act of the Dolomites story: the Athesian Volcanic Group, the largest Permian volcanic district in Europe [@chiaro-2024]. Its rocks range from basaltic andesites to rhyolites, and zircon ages show that the volcanism lasted about ten million years, roughly between 285 and 274 million years ago [@marocchi-2008]. Its final eruption, the Ora Ignimbrite, was of supereruption size [@chiaro-2024].
