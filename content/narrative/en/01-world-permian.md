Three hundred million years ago the Earth held a single continent. The marker for the future Dolomites sits deep inside it, close to the equator, on the piece of shelf we now call Adria. Paleomagnetic data show that this margin, the Dolomites included, has moved in close step with Africa since at least Permian times [@muttoni-2001].

The globe replays the PALEOMAP reconstruction of Scotese & Wright 2018, one model for the whole span from three hundred million years ago to today. The coarse land-and-sea texture behind the plates, the PaleoDEM at its native five-million-year steps, comes from the same reconstruction. The model draws plate boundaries only for the last hundred million years, so none appear in this chapter [@scotese-2018].

Under the marker lie the eroded roots of the older Variscan mountains. On top of them sits the first act of the Dolomites story: the Athesian Volcanic Group, the largest Permian volcanic district in Europe [@chiaro-2024]. Its rocks range from basaltic andesites to rhyolites, and zircon ages show that the volcanism lasted about ten million years, roughly between 285 and 274 million years ago [@marocchi-2008]. The final eruption, the Ora Ignimbrite, was of supereruption size [@chiaro-2024].
